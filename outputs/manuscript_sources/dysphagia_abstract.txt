Swallowing problems may affect social participation, but their relationship with subjective loneliness remains incompletely characterized. We examined this association among US adults aged 65 years or older using cross-sectional data from the 2025 National Health Interview Survey. Exposure was a self-reported swallowing problem during the previous 12 months; frequent loneliness meant always or usually feeling lonely. Survey-weighted modified Poisson regression accounted for the complex sampling design and estimated prevalence ratios. The primary model adjusted for demographic, socioeconomic, household, and health characteristics and in-person and telephone/video contact. Estimates were pooled across ten official income imputations. Logistic standardization provided adjusted absolute estimates. Among 8,231 respondents with valid exposure and outcome data, weighted frequent loneliness prevalence was 10.34% with swallowing problems and 4.17% without. The common regression sample included 8,094 respondents. The primary adjusted prevalence ratio was 1.83 (95% confidence interval 1.42–2.38). Standardized prevalence was 7.94% versus 4.27%, with a difference of 3.67 percentage points (95% confidence interval 1.75–5.60). Adjusted prevalence ratios increased across reported swallowing-difficulty categories. Additional adjustment for frequent depressive feelings attenuated the association, whereas response weighting produced similar estimates. Self-reported swallowing problems were associated with frequent loneliness after adjustment for measured health and social characteristics. These cross-sectional findings do not establish temporal direction or causality. Longitudinal studies should distinguish perceived loneliness from contact frequency and evaluate the social and emotional consequences of swallowing problems.
